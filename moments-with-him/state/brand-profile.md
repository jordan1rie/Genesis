## Brand Voice

Moments With Him writes to a man who already believes — the ad is never trying to convert him, only to close the gap between what he believes and how he actually spends a Tuesday afternoon. Rules, pulled directly from the brand's own research on this reader:

- **Mission language over relationship language.** Say "show up," not "grow closer." Say "follow me," not "walk with Him." This reader is allergic to soft, relational, everyone-welcome framing — it reads as written for someone else.
- **No guilt, ever.** The single highest-converting proof point in the brand's own data is "a devotional that doesn't make me feel like a failure when I miss a day." Never build urgency or motivation out of shame — name the private feeling, then offer relief, never the other way around.
- **Never lead with hellfire or eternal stakes.** The market's own vocabulary is numb, static, roommates, lukewarm, incomplete, a shadow, still failing — not damnation. Write in that register.
- **First-person, confessional, plainspoken.** Short sentences. Blue-collar, not seminary. He "hates to read" — copy earns every line, no padding.
- **The gap is the hook.** Pair a named, specific pain with a mechanism that seems too small to fix it ("one page," "60 seconds") — that size mismatch is the curiosity engine. Don't close the gap too early.
- **Fear for his kids, not for himself.** The strongest lever in the brand's competitor research (Men's Fraternity: "the crisis we see in men always goes back to the dad they grew up with or without") is legacy fear — what gets passed down — not fear for his own soul.
- **No fabricated urgency.** No countdown timers, no fake scarcity. This reader is specifically wary of anything that smells like manipulation. Real triggers only: Father's Day, New Year, a genuine "start on page one whenever you order" mechanic.
- **Modest, not premium, positioning.** This avatar skews middle-to-working class. No aspirational-wealth imagery, no luxury framing.
- **Always close with the 30-day money-back guarantee**, positioned near the offer, not buried — it's the brand's strongest answer to "I've tried and failed at devotionals before."

## Winning Ad 1: The 50-Minute Soccer Problem

**Awareness Level:** Unaware

Here's a question I can't stop thinking about, and I want you to sit with it for a second before you get defensive.

Why is it that a man can watch 50 minutes of soccer — or football, or a fishing show, or whatever your thing is — without checking his watch once… but 20 minutes with the Word of God feels like it lasts a week?

Same man. Same attention span. Same guy who can quote stats from a game that happened three years ago.

I'm not saying it to make you feel bad. I'm saying it because it points to something almost nobody talks about, and it's not what you think.

It's not that you're lazy. It's not that you don't believe. Most men I'm describing here would tell you flat out — Jesus is their Savior, no hesitation, they'd say it in front of anyone. That's not the gap.

The gap is this: somewhere along the way, faith became something you *perform* instead of something you *live*. You pray with the kids because they're watching. You show up to church and half of it washes over you like static — you're in the seat, but you're not really hearing anything back. You do it for them.

And here's the part nobody warns you about. Being in that in-between place — believing but not living it — doesn't feel like crisis. It feels like *nothing*. You're not miserable. You're just… numb. Comfortable, but not happy. You've got the house, the truck, the paycheck, the family looking okay on paper — and this quiet hum underneath all of it that something's off and you can't name it.

There's an old line about a church that said "I am rich and I need nothing" — and the answer back was, "you don't realize you're poor, blind, and naked." That's the lukewarm place. And it's sneakier than being cold, because a cold man knows he's cold.

The good news? Closing that gap doesn't take 20 minutes. It doesn't take a plan you'll quit by Thursday. There's a way men are doing it in about the time it takes to pour a cup of coffee — one page, once a day — and it's changing the way they walk into their own homes.

I'll tell you what it is next. For now, just answer the soccer question honestly.

## Winning Ad 2: The Guy Who Hates to Read

**Awareness Level:** Problem-Aware

Let me guess.

You've tried the Bible. You really have. You'll pick it up with good intentions and by the third paragraph you're thinking, *I have no idea what this is actually saying,* and it quietly ends up back on the shelf.

Maybe you tried a devotional too. And it was worse — pages and pages, more to process than you'll ever hold onto. By the time you're pulling out of the driveway, every word of it is already gone from your head. There was good stuff in there, probably. You just couldn't carry any of it into your actual day.

So you did what most men do. You told yourself you don't have the time.

Here's the honest truth: it was never really about time. You'll find 50 minutes for a game. It's about the fact that every option in front of you felt like *one more thing you're already failing at* — one more long, drawn-out assignment built for someone with a quiet house and a free hour every morning. That's not you. You've got kids, a job that empties the tank, and a phone that eats the leftover minutes before you notice they're gone.

You don't need more material. You need *less.* Something short enough to actually remember. Something you can chew on at a red light or between meetings instead of forgetting the second you close the cover.

And — this matters — something made for *you.* A man. Not a devotional written to "everyone" that never quite fits. One that talks about the stuff men actually carry: the numbness, the going-through-the-motions, the wanting to be the kind of father your kids could look up to and not knowing where to even start.

That's a different kind of thing than what you've tried before. Not a reading program. Not a homework pile. Something you could keep in the truck, read in about a minute, and still feel it working on you all day.

If you've ever said "I honestly hate to read" — this was built with you in mind. Keep reading. I'll show you exactly what it is.

## Winning Ad 3: The Static on the Line

**Awareness Level:** Problem-Aware

You still pray. Maybe every day.

But if you're honest — really honest, the kind of honest you don't say out loud to another man — it feels like talking into static. You send it up, and nothing comes back. It's a habit now more than a conversation. And you can't remember the last time it felt like anything at all.

That's the thing about drifting. It doesn't happen in a dramatic moment. You didn't decide to walk away. Life just got loud. Work. Bills. The kids. The endless scroll on your phone at 11pm. And the faith you grew up with — the one you'd still defend if anyone challenged it — slowly became background noise you stopped actually listening to.

Here's what almost nobody tells you about that place: you can't feel how empty it is *while you're in it.* You're too busy, too distracted, too comfortable. It doesn't hurt. It just goes quiet. And quiet is easy to ignore — until the day your kid asks you a real question about God and you realize you'd be faking your answer.

You don't want that. You don't want your kids looking back one day thinking, *I wish my dad had done it God's way.*

So let me name the real problem, because it's not what you think it is. It's not that you don't believe. It's not even that you don't have time. It's that everything you've reached for to fix it demanded more than you had to give — a big block of morning, a book you'd never finish, a plan that made you feel like a failure by day four.

The gap between what you believe and how you live isn't closed with more effort. It's closed with *consistency* — something so small you can't talk yourself out of it, but real enough to cut through the static.

There's a way men are doing exactly that, one page at a time, and it starts working on marriages and on the way a man walks into his own house faster than most of them expected. Keep reading — this one's for the man who's tired of praying into silence.

## Winning Ad 4: What Nobody Says About the Comfortable Man

**Awareness Level:** Unaware

There's a certain kind of man everyone assumes is fine.

He pays the bills. He shows up to the games. He's still married, still goes to church most Sundays, still prays. On paper he's a good dad and a good man, and nobody in his life would say otherwise.

And he is quietly rotting from the inside out.

I know that's a hard sentence. But it's the one thing nobody will say to his face, because from the outside there's nothing to fix. He looks content. He *is* comfortable. What he isn't — is happy. There's a hole in the middle of him that the paycheck didn't fill, that the promotion didn't fill, that even the family he loves can't quite fill. He's spent years grinding at a career he's good at and it still leaves him feeling numb.

Here's the strange part. He'd tell you he believes in God. He means it. So why the emptiness?

Because belief that never gets *lived* turns into static. And a man in that state is in a more dangerous place than a man who's angry at the whole thing — because the angry man knows something's wrong. The comfortable man doesn't. He mistakes numb for fine. He thinks because he doesn't steal, doesn't cheat anyone big, doesn't do anything *serious* — that he's good. That he doesn't need to change anything.

That's the trap. Not sin. Complacency. Being lukewarm — neither hot nor cold — is the one condition that doesn't set off any alarms while it slowly hollows a man out.

Now here's the good news, and it's genuinely good: no matter how far a man's drifted, how numb he's gotten, how long he's been coasting — the way back isn't complicated. It isn't a mountain of reading. It isn't a guilt trip. It's smaller than that. Almost embarrassingly small.

It's about 60 seconds a day. One page. A question, a thought, and God's own answer written right there so you don't have to go digging.

If you recognized yourself in "comfortable but not happy" — that recognition is the first thing that's woken up in you in a long time. Don't let it go back to sleep. I'll show you what those 60 seconds look like.

## Winning Ad 5: The Man Your Kids Are Watching

**Awareness Level:** Problem-Aware

You want to know what actually keeps a man like you up at night? It's not the mortgage.

It's the quiet fear that your kids are going to grow up, look back, and think: *I wish my dad had been a Godly man. I wish he'd done it God's way.*

You'd never say that out loud. But it's in there. Because you know the difference between providing for your kids and *leading* them. You've got the first one handled — the bills are paid, the fridge is full, you show up. It's the second one you keep telling yourself you'll get to. The spiritual leader part. The example part. The part where you're supposed to be the man they look up to for support, not just the guy who covers the expenses.

And every day you don't, that gap gets a little wider. Your wife notices it before you do. Somewhere in the last few years the two of you drifted from partners into roommates — polite, functional, distant. You feel it. You just don't know how to name what's missing, and "read the Bible more" has never once actually happened because who has an hour and the energy after a day like yours?

So here's the reframe. You've been treating this like a *time* problem. It isn't. It's a *starting* problem. Every path you've considered — the whole Bible, the thick devotional, the reading plan — asks you to become a different kind of man overnight. Nobody does that. That's why you've quit every time.

But leadership in a home doesn't start with an hour. It starts with a minute a man actually keeps. Wives have watched it happen — a husband starts something small and daily, and within weeks the attitude shifts, the conversations get deeper, he starts sharing what he's learning without being asked. The static clears. The roommate starts becoming a husband again.

Your kids don't need you to become a preacher. They need to catch you, one ordinary morning, actually seeking God — and know it's real.

There's a way to start that today, in about 60 seconds, built specifically for men who never thought they had time for it. Keep reading. This is the one you don't want to put off another year.
